### I Didn't Fight the Pain, I Fought the Unknown

It's a sweltering July, and I often feel like a turtle in a shell without a fan. The pain is always on high alert, a constant, frustrating, and maddening presence. I've mentioned before that I use a five-point pain scale, which I explain in my **[pain diary](http://livingwithpain.co.uk)**.

### My Struggle with Trigeminal Neuralgia

To survive, I practice mindful breathing to regulate my nervous system, and I massage the affected area of my face. But the nerve won't give up, and my brain, despite my best efforts, focuses on that spot and tries to overwhelm me.

I'm exhausted and bombarded with thoughts about pain and living with neuropathy. I think back to the early years of my illness, when I spent hours reading everything I could find about **trigeminal neuralgia**. I would sit in front of my monitor, almost screaming:

*   This isn't right!
*   This isn't my pain!
*   What's wrong with me?!

I wasn't just asking these questions to an unresponsive screen; I was asking them to every doctor I met.

What puzzles me the most is that no one told me about neuropathy back then. No one explained that a damaged nerve could cause constant pain, that the nervous system could become oversensitive, and that it didn't mean I was crazy or that my body was inventing its own suffering. I thought I was an exception for a long time.

It wasn't until years later that I began to understand that the answers weren't just in the damaged nerve. That's just the beginning of the story. What's much more important is what happens next. The brain is responsible for our safety and constantly monitors both our body and environment.

When I was looking for an image that best represented this extraordinary vigilance, I thought of the Lord of the Rings. I imagine my brain like the Eye of Sauron. After losing the Ring, Sauron no longer had a physical body, but his consciousness was always on the lookout for what he considered most important.

From the high tower of Barad-dûr, his burning eye constantly scanned the world, searching for the slightest sign of the Ring, where he had hidden his power. It never gave up. Every signal was analyzed, every possibility checked. Its primary goal was to regain what would ensure the survival of his power.

Our brain works in a surprisingly similar way. Of course, it's not evil or cruel. On the contrary, its only task is to keep us alive.

Through millions of years of evolution, it has developed a system that constantly scans our surroundings and body for threats. It analyzes temperature, touch, muscle tension, sounds, smells, memories, emotions, and signals from every nerve. All this is done to detect danger as early as possible and react before it becomes a real threat.

That's why pain exists. Not to punish us, but to protect us. How fascinating and misunderstood pain is – let's leave that for another post.

The problem arises when, after nerve damage, this protective system starts working too zealously. The brain, which once learned that a signal of danger comes from a particular place, doesn't want to let go.

Like the Eye of Sauron, it returns its gaze to the same spot again and again, convinced that's where danger might lurk. The longer this state lasts, the more the entire nervous system becomes sensitized. Weaker stimuli are interpreted as something that requires an alarm.

The paradox is that the brain doesn't work against us; it's just that, because it's trying so hard to protect us, it sometimes triggers an alarm that won't quiet down.

For many years, I tried to understand why my body wouldn't turn off this alarm. Today, I know much more. And it's this knowledge that brings me great regret. Not that I got sick, but that for years, I didn't have the opportunity to confront the real image of my illness.

No one told me what neuropathic pain was or how it could progress. No one explained that there were ways to work with chronic pain beyond just prescriptions and changing medications. I had to figure many things out on my own, after years of reading research, books, and talking to people who had similar experiences.

That's what hurts the most. I visited many specialists, including those who deal with pain management every day. And yet, for a long time, I didn't receive the knowledge that could have helped me better understand my experience.

Today, I'm sure that neuropathic pain shouldn't be left to chance. Especially when pharmacology proves insufficient or can't be used. And especially for people who live with pain for so long that it becomes part of their daily life and leaves a lasting mark on the functioning of their nervous system.

I won't get back the lost years. Still, I want to believe that even after so many years, the brain can learn, and one day, this alarm won't sound with such force.

I'm also convinced that if I had understood the nature of my illness earlier, I would have spared myself many years of powerlessness. Much sooner, I would have stopped looking for fault in myself if I had heard earlier that my pain wasn't an exception but a consequence of nerve damage.

Despite everything, I won't stop believing in the possibilities of our brain. Not because I believe in miracles, but because I understand the neuroplasticity of our brain, that evolutionary wonder. The ability of the nervous system to change and learn throughout life gives me hope that even after many years, my nervous system can learn new, safer ways to react.

I don't know if I'll ever completely free myself from pain. Maybe not. But I know I don't want to stop teaching my brain that not every experience is a threat and that not every signal needs to trigger an alarm with such force.

If the nervous system could learn to live in constant readiness over the years, then I want to believe that with patience, proper knowledge, and daily work, it can also learn a greater sense of security.

That's the hope I want to hold onto, not because it means everything will be okay, but because it gives me the willingness to move forward despite uncertainty. This hope is based on the fact that as long as the brain can learn, it's worth trying.
