Facial pain associated with trigeminal nerve damage can take many forms, from paroxysmal, electric discharges in neuralgia to continuous, burning discomfort in neuropathy.

Both types of pain can be difficult to manage, and treatment often requires a combination of methods.

While medications and procedures remain the standard, we increasingly seek supportive approaches that can ease pain and improve daily functioning. One of these is acupuncture, a technique known for thousands of years that is now also being examined by modern medicine and science.

Can it truly help with trigeminal pain? Let us look at what both East and West have to say.

## Advantages of Acupuncture

- It may reduce pain intensity and attack frequency in some patients, particularly as an adjunct to pharmacological treatment.
- It is gentle, generally well tolerated, and has few side effects, especially compared to anticonvulsants.
- It helps some people reduce tension, improve sleep, and decrease pain-related anxiety, increasing the sense of control over the disease.

## Limitations and Drawbacks

- Scientific evidence is not conclusive. Many studies have small sample sizes, limited blinding, and varied acupuncture protocols.
- Acupuncture does not replace causal treatment (e.g., microvascular decompression for neurovascular conflict) or medication. It should be a complementary element.
- Effectiveness varies. Some people experience no clinically meaningful improvement.

## The Eastern Medicine Perspective

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), facial pain represents a disruption in the flow of "Qi" energy and blood in the facial and head meridians.

Acupuncture aims to "unblock" flow, restore balance, and reduce pain.

Most commonly used points include:

- Hegu (LI4) — between the thumb and index finger,
- Taichong (LR3) — on the foot,
- Xiaguan (ST7) and Sibai (ST2) — on the face.

Practitioners use various techniques: classical manual needling, electroacupuncture (gentle current pulses), and moxibustion (heat therapy).

The goal is not only pain relief but also harmonization of the nervous and myofascial systems, which may improve overall well-being.

## The Western Medicine Perspective

From a neurophysiological standpoint, acupuncture may work through:

- activation of descending pain inhibition pathways in the central nervous system,
- release of endogenous opioids (β-endorphins, enkephalins),
- effects on neurotransmitters (serotonin, noradrenaline),
- modulation of pain processing in the cerebral cortex.

The effect is therefore more neuromodulatory than mysterious. It involves enhancing the body's natural pain inhibition mechanisms.

## What the Research Shows

Large reviews (30 studies, over 2,200 patients) show that acupuncture may reduce pain and attacks more than the standard drug **carbamazepine**, with fewer side effects. However, the quality of these studies is low, so we cannot be entirely certain of the results.

A meta-analysis of 16 studies (over 1,200 patients) presents a similar picture: acupuncture helps reduce pain with fewer side effects than medications alone. But here too, evidence is very limited, and large, well-designed trials are needed.

The most recent reviews (2023–2024) show a "promising signal," particularly for electroacupuncture, but note that points and techniques vary considerably, making definitive conclusions difficult.

Clinical observations and patient reports indicate that acupuncture can decrease attack frequency and intensity and improve quality of life, especially when used alongside medication.

**Conclusion:** Acupuncture is not a miracle cure, but for many people it can be valuable support in the fight against facial pain.

## Who Should Consider It and When

- When medications are poorly tolerated or do not provide sufficient relief.
- As support after neurosurgical procedures or before deciding on surgery.
- In neuropathies with constant pain, as part of a broader program: education, physical activity, psychological therapy, and sleep hygiene.

## Safety and Practice

- Always choose a certified therapist working in sterile conditions and collaborating with your treating physician.
- Typically a series of sessions is recommended (1–2 times per week for several weeks).
- Keep a pain diary and evaluate results after completing the cycle.

**Warning signs:** worsening attacks, facial numbness, speech or swallowing disturbances require urgent medical consultation.

## How to Discuss It with Your Doctor

- Ask whether acupuncture is compatible with your treatment plan.
- Set specific, measurable goals: e.g., "reduce attack frequency by 30% in 6 weeks" or reduce constant pain.
- Remember: the best results are achieved when traditional and modern approaches collaborate rather than compete.
