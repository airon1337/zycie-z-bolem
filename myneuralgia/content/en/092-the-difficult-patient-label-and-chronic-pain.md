## The "problem patient" with chronic pain

Modern medicine can transplant organs, perform complex neurosurgical operations, and treat diseases that were a death sentence just decades ago. And yet, in the shadow of these spectacular successes, there remains a group of patients who don't fit into simple diagnostic algorithms — patients with chronic pain. In clinical practice, they are often labeled "difficult," "demanding," "time-consuming." In the informal language of the system: problematic. That's how we feel.

Let's try to examine the patient-physician relationship from the perspective of someone experiencing chronic pain, and try to understand why the pain patient so often finds themselves at the margins of medical attention.

## Pain that cannot be seen

Chronic pain differs fundamentally from acute pain. It is not an alarm signal indicating a fresh injury or inflammation. It often becomes an autonomous phenomenon — a consequence of nerve damage, disrupted stimulus processing, or consolidated changes in the nervous system. In trigeminal neuralgia or neuropathy, pain may feel like a toothache, even though the problem involves a neural structure.

For the patient, this means living in constant tension, uncertainty, and fluctuating intensity of suffering. For the physician — a case that offers no quick, clear-cut answers.

## What kind of relationship emerges?

The patient-physician relationship is inherently asymmetric. The physician has knowledge, authority, and decision-making power. The patient brings symptoms, fear, and hope for help. In the paternalistic model, the doctor "knows" and the patient "complies."

The problem begins when the patient's experience doesn't fit textbook patterns. When they report atypical symptoms, connect facts differently, suggest a possible link between an infection and nerve pain — and their voice is ignored. Lack of interest, unwillingness to listen, lack of time to review additional information — these are signals that the relationship is not a partnership.

In this dynamic, the patient stops being a participant in the treatment process and becomes an element of procedure.

## "Difficult patient" or difficult medicine?

In the medical community, the pain patient is sometimes perceived as:

- Demanding
- Overly focused on symptoms
- Requiring long consultations
- Dissatisfied with treatment outcomes

But from the other side, we should ask: is it the patient who is difficult, or is it that medicine doesn't always have the tools to deal with chronic pain?

Pain that doesn't appear on imaging, that produces no definitive lab results, places the physician in a position of uncertainty. And uncertainty can be uncomfortable. Sometimes it's easier to classify a patient as "problematic" than to admit: "I don't know" or "We need to broaden our perspective."

## When patients become their own advocates

One of the most painful aspects of the patient-physician relationship is the moment when the patient begins playing the role of educator. Searching for studies, reading articles, warning others, suggesting possible diagnoses. In pain, in exhaustion, in a sense of threat — trying to save themselves and others.

This is a role reversal. The healthcare system should provide safety and diagnostic guidance. When patients feel they are entirely on their own, trust is lost. And trust is the foundation of effective therapy.

For someone living with pain, it is also critically important that their experience is recognized as real. Even if medicine doesn't yet have a definitive answer, simply listening and taking the patient's account seriously has enormous therapeutic significance. A lack of validation leads to secondary suffering — alongside physical pain comes psychological pain: the sense of being invisible, misunderstood, ignored. The patient begins to doubt themselves, their perceptions, and whether there's any point in continuing to seek help.

## The system behind the relationship

The patient-physician relationship doesn't exist in a vacuum. It's embedded in a system that limits appointment times, rewards procedures, doesn't always encourage interdisciplinary collaboration, and rarely creates space to analyze atypical cases. The pain patient requires time, attentiveness, and often the cooperation of multiple specialists. Meanwhile, the system tends to favor speed and standardization. As a result, difficult cases are pushed to the margins, and the experience they bring doesn't translate into clinical knowledge development.

## Patients as partners

Paradoxically, it is patients with chronic pain who possess unique knowledge — embodied knowledge arising from daily experience of symptoms. They can precisely describe the character of pain, aggravating and relieving factors, and medication responses.

In an ideal partnership model, physician and patient form a team where academic knowledge meets experiential knowledge. In such an arrangement, not only is treatment more effective, but medicine itself advances through the analysis of atypical cases.

The pain patient is not a problematic patient by definition. They are a patient who requires time, empathy, openness, and humility in the face of the complexity of the human body.

A relationship that can bring real relief should be based on:

- Dialogue rather than unilateral decisions
- Recognition of the patient's experience
- Willingness to acknowledge uncertainty
- Shared responsibility for the treatment process

In a world where medicine achieves ever-greater technological precision, we cannot forget that at its center remains a person. A patient with chronic pain doesn't need the label "difficult." They need safety, to be heard, and genuine engagement.

Perhaps the greatest challenge is not pain itself, but a shift in perspective — from viewing the patient as a problem to recognizing them as a partner in the treatment process. Only then can the patient-physician relationship become a space where suffering is not only diagnosed but also truly understood.
