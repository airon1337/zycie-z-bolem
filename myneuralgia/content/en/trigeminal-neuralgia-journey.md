## Introduction to My Journey

The sweltering heat of July has arrived. Sometimes, I feel like a turtle in a shell without a fan, with my pain always on high alert, at that unpleasant, frustrating, and infuriating level. I've mentioned before that I use a five-point pain scale? I explain why in my [Pain Diary](http://livingwithpain.co.uk), which can be downloaded from the website.

## The Beginning of My Reflections

Going back to my cruel experiences with this small part of my face, to survive, I breathe consciously, regulating my nervous system, and massage the painful area, but the nerve won't give up, and my brain, despite my efforts, focuses on that spot and tries to exhaust me...

Tired and bombarded, I think more about the pain, about life with neuropathy. I go back in memory to the first years of living with pain, to the beginning of my illness. When I spent hours reading everything I could find about **trigeminal neuralgia**. I sat in front of the monitor and almost screamed:

– But that's not how it is!
– That's not my pain!
– What's wrong with me?!

In reality, I asked these questions not only to the silent screen. I asked them to every doctor I met on my way.

What puzzles me the most is that no one told me about neuropathy back then. No one explained that a damaged nerve can cause constant pain, that the nervous system can become oversensitive, and that it doesn't mean I'm crazy or that my body is inventing its suffering. For a long time, I thought I was an exception.

## The Brain Like the Eye of Sauron

Only after years did I start to understand that the answers shouldn't be sought only in the damaged nerve. That's just the beginning of the story. Much more important is what happens later. It's the brain that's responsible for our safety and constantly monitors both the body and the environment. When I was looking for an image that would best illustrate this extraordinary vigilance, I remembered The Lord of the Rings.

Thanks to this amazing novel, I imagine the brain a bit like the Eye of Sauron. After losing the Ring, Sauron no longer had a physical body, but his consciousness constantly watched for what he considered most important. From the high tower of Barad-dûr, his burning eye constantly scanned the world, searching for the slightest sign of the Ring, where he had hidden his power. It never gave up. Every signal was analyzed, every possibility checked. His most important goal was to regain what would ensure the survival of his power.

Our brain works in a surprisingly similar way. Of course, it's not evil or cruel. On the contrary, its only task is to keep us alive.

Through millions of years of evolution, it has developed a system that constantly scans the environment and our body for threats. It analyzes temperature, touch, muscle tension, sounds, smells, memories, emotions, and signals from every nerve. All this to detect danger as early as possible and react before it becomes a real threat. That's why pain exists. Not to punish us, but to protect us. How fascinating and at the same time misunderstood pain is, we'll leave for another post.

The problem arises when, after nerve damage, this protective system starts working too zealously. The brain, which once learned that a signal of danger comes from a particular place, doesn't want to give up. Like the Eye of Sauron, it returns its gaze to the same spot again and again, convinced that danger might be lurking there. The longer this state lasts, the more the entire nervous system becomes sensitized. Weaker stimuli are interpreted as something that requires an alarm.

The paradox is that the brain doesn't work against us, but precisely because it tries to protect us with all its might, it sometimes triggers an alarm that won't quiet down.

## What Was Missing at the Beginning of My Illness

For many years, I tried to understand why my body wouldn't turn off this alarm. Today, I know much more. And it's this knowledge that brings me great regret. Not that I got sick, but that for years I didn't have the opportunity to confront the real picture of my illness.

No one told me what neuropathic pain is and how it can progress. No one explained that there are ways to work with chronic pain that go beyond just prescriptions and changing medications. I had to figure many things out on my own, after years of reading research, books, and talking to people who had similar experiences.

That's what hurts the most. I visited many specialists, including those who deal with pain management on a daily basis. And yet, for a long time, I didn't receive the knowledge that could have helped me better understand my own experience.

Today, I'm sure that neuropathic pain shouldn't be left to chance. Especially when pharmacology proves insufficient or can't be used. And especially in people who live with pain for so long that it becomes part of their daily life and leaves a lasting mark on the functioning of the nervous system.

## Neuroplasticity Gives Me Hope

I won't get back the lost years. Still, I want to believe that even after so many years, the brain can learn, and that one day this alarm won't sound with such force.

I'm also convinced that if I had understood the nature of my illness earlier, I would have spared myself many years of powerlessness. Much earlier, I would have stopped looking for fault in myself if I had heard that my pain wasn't an exception, but a consequence of damage to the nervous system.

Despite everything, I won't stop believing in the possibilities of our brain. Not because I believe in miracles, but because I understand the neuroplasticity of our brain, this evolutionary wonder. The ability of the nervous system to change and learn throughout life. It's what allows us to learn to walk, talk, ride a bike, or recover after a stroke. This same property gives me hope that even after many years, my nervous system can learn new, safer ways of reacting.

I don't know if I'll ever completely free myself from pain. Maybe not. But I know I don't want to stop teaching my brain that not every experience is a threat and that not every signal must trigger the alarm with the same force.

If the nervous system could learn to live in constant readiness over the years, then I want to believe that with patience, proper knowledge, and daily work, it can also learn a greater sense of security.

That's the hope I want to hold onto, because it doesn't mean believing everything will be okay, but being ready to move forward despite uncertainty. This hope is based on the fact that as long as the brain can learn, it's worth trying.
