The Trigeminal Nerve Explained — The Largest Cranial Nerve You Never Think About

Author: Natalia · Date: 2025-09-25 · 2 min read
Category: Symptoms & Diagnosis

Most of us have no idea how much of our daily functioning depends on the trigeminal nerve (nervus trigeminus). It is the fifth and largest cranial nerve, responsible for almost the entire face.

It is a mixed nerve — meaning it combines both sensory and motor fibers. It not only allows us to feel, but also drives certain muscles.

Three Major Branches

The trigeminal nerve is divided into three large branches:

  • Ophthalmic (V1) — receives stimuli from the eye, forehead, and scalp.
  • Maxillary (V2) — responsible for sensation in the cheeks, nose, and upper teeth.
  • Mandibular (V3) — contains both sensory and motor fibers; it covers the lower teeth, tongue, and the muscles of mastication.

What the Trigeminal Nerve Does

Its range of function is truly impressive:

  • transmits sensory stimuli from the skin of the face, eyes, nasal cavity, sinuses, and oral cavity,
  • provides sensation to the teeth and part of the tongue,
  • controls the lacrimal glands,
  • drives the muscles of mastication — enabling us to bite into an apple, crack a nut, or enjoy a piece of chocolate.

Why This Matters

The trigeminal nerve works like the face's central switchboard — receiving and relaying an enormous number of signals. It is thanks to this nerve that we feel a gust of wind, taste our favorite foods, sense a touch, or can smile while chewing something delicious.

When the nerve is functioning normally, we never give it a second thought. But when inflammation or neuralgia develops, we quickly realize just how enormous a role it plays.

A Fun Fact

The nerve gets its name — "trigeminal" — precisely because it divides into three main branches that, like a network of electrical wires, envelop the face and serve its different regions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

Which doctor should I see for facial pain?

First, see a neurologist. If surgery is being considered, a neurosurgeon. If a dental cause is suspected, it is also worth ruling it out with a dentist.
