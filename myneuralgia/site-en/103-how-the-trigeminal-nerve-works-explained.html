How the Trigeminal Nerve Works — And Why It Matters When It Doesn't

Author: Natalia · Date: 2026-03-29 · 4 min read
Category: Symptoms & Diagnosis

A video illustrating how the trigeminal nerve works has been circulating online for the past few days — one of the most important nerves in our body, responsible for facial sensation, protective reflexes, and the muscles of chewing. I want to share it with you because it shows very visually something that many of us face every single day. Below is a translation and summary of its content.

The trigeminal nerve: the face's sensory system

The largest cranial nerve is the trigeminal nerve — a key structure responsible for sensation, control, and rapid responses in the face. It branches into three main divisions, covering the skin of the face, the scalp, and the mandible. It conducts signals from the skin, teeth, and deeper structures.

With every touch, temperature change, or pressure, sensory receptors are activated, generating electrical impulses that travel along the nerve to the brain. They reach nuclei in the brainstem where they are processed and interpreted as pain, touch, texture, or warmth. This is how the face perceives stimuli from the environment.

However, the trigeminal nerve's function is not limited to sensation alone. Its motor component controls the muscles of mastication, enabling precise jaw movements — opening, closing, and generating force. It is also responsible for protective reflexes. A sudden stimulus, such as a sharp touch, high temperature, or eye irritation, can trigger an immediate response — like blinking or pulling away — even before the stimulus is consciously registered.

Due to its extensive reach, the trigeminal nerve remains constantly active — monitoring, processing, and coordinating information from one of the most sensitive areas of the body. When its function is disrupted, signals can become amplified, misinterpreted, or trigger pain even with minimal stimulation.

Fast, extensive, and deeply integrated — the trigeminal nerve is not just an impulse pathway. It is a complex facial sensory system, a movement controller, and an instant-response mechanism that directly connects the external world to the brain.

What this means for those of us living with pain

I've often imagined what this nerve actually looks like and how it works. What exactly happens "in there" when pain appears, or when an ordinary touch suddenly stops being ordinary. This video captures that very well.

It shows something that's hard to put into words: that this is not just "a nerve," but an entire network that is vast, fast, and constantly active. And that when it works properly, we don't even notice it. Only when something begins to change does it suddenly become the central point of daily life.

For us, living with facial pain, this isn't an abstraction. These are the moments when a light touch, a gust of wind, or washing your face can trigger a response completely out of proportion to the stimulus. And that's very difficult to explain to someone who hasn't experienced it.

This animation shows — though unfortunately too briefly — that this isn't a matter of "exaggeration," but the real way the nervous system processes information. Signals can be amplified, distorted, or appear without a clear cause.

What's also important to me is that this nerve works all the time — it cannot be "switched off" or rested from. The face is constantly exposed to stimuli, and the body continuously receives and interprets them.

That's why understanding matters — both for ourselves and for others. Because behind something that is often invisible, there is a very concrete mechanism.

If you feel that this material shows what often cannot be explained in words — share it further. The more people see what this pain truly looks like, the easier it will be for us to talk about it and be understood.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

Which doctor should I see for facial pain?

First, see a neurologist. If surgery is being considered, a neurosurgeon. If a dental cause is suspected, it is also worth ruling it out with a dentist.
