How to Keep a Pain Diary — A Practical Guide for Chronic Pain

Author: Natalia · Date: 2025-10-30 · 3 min read
Category: Living with Pain

Living with chronic pain is a daily battle that is difficult for anyone who has never fought it to understand.

Sometimes the pain is constant, sometimes paroxysmal, sometimes quiet and drawn out, and sometimes sudden, paralyzing, and breath-taking.

In the flood of days, medications, emotions, and attempts at functioning, it is easy to lose track.

This is where a pain diary helps.

What Is a Pain Diary?

It is a tool that helps you track, analyze, and understand your pain.

It is not just a notebook with attack times. It is a personal record of experience that can become invaluable support for your doctor, psychologist, and above all, for yourself or someone close to you who is dealing with chronic pain.

In a pain diary you record all the most important information related to pain episodes:

  • time and duration of pain,
  • pain intensity (e.g., on a 0–10 scale, or a four-level verbal scale: 0 = no pain, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe),
  • type of pain (stabbing, burning, throbbing, piercing, etc.),
  • location (e.g., right side of face, around the eye, jaw),
  • triggering factors (cold, touch, stress, noise, eating),
  • alleviating factors (medication, rest, warmth, sleep),
  • accompanying symptoms (tearing, numbness, tinnitus, fatigue, dizziness),
  • medications taken and their effectiveness,
  • emotional well-being that day.

How to Start Keeping a Pain Diary

You do not need anything complicated.

A simple notebook, a phone app, a calendar, or a computer spreadsheet is enough.

What matters most is regularity and honesty.

Step 1. Choose a format that is comfortable for you. Some prefer paper because it allows them to "talk it out," which works best for me personally, but notes on a phone or computer also work well.

Step 2. Decide what you want to track. Not everyone needs to record everything. Choose the most important items: time of attack, intensity, what triggered it, how long it lasted, what helped, or what emotions accompanied you that day.

Step 3. Record daily. Even if you have no pain, note that too. This helps you understand remission periods, protective factors, and lifestyle effects.

Step 4. Review your entries once a week. Look for patterns: perhaps attacks worsen after a sleepless night, after stress, after a weather change, or after specific foods.

Why It Is Worth Keeping a Pain Diary

  • It helps you understand your body — over time you begin to see patterns that were previously invisible.
  • It gives a sense of control — pain stops being entirely "random"; you can see when and why it appears.
  • It helps in communication with loved ones — it is easier to explain why on a certain day you cannot talk, and on another you have more energy.
  • It supports mental health — writing down pain is also a form of "releasing" emotions. The diary becomes a space where you can name fear, frustration, and hope without judgment.
  • It helps your doctor — detailed records make it easier to select treatment, assess medication effectiveness, and avoid unnecessary trials.

Tips

  • Do not be afraid to record emotions: anger, sadness, fear. They are part of the pain experience.
  • Mark pain-free days too. That is also important data.
  • If pain is paroxysmal (as in trigeminal neuralgia), record each attack separately.
  • If pain is continuous, note changes in intensity throughout the day.

The most important thing is to write honestly and consistently, not perfectly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

What is a pain diary and why keep one?

It is a record of pain attacks: when they occur, what triggers them, how long they last, and what medications you take. It greatly helps your doctor in making a diagnosis and choosing treatment.

Which doctor should I see for facial pain?

First, see a neurologist. If surgery is being considered, a neurosurgeon. If a dental cause is suspected, it is also worth ruling it out with a dentist.

Can trigeminal neuralgia be cured?

In many people, pain can be effectively controlled with medications or procedures, and MVD provides the most durable results in eligible patients. The course varies — with periods of remission and relapse. Decisions are made together with your doctor.
