Automatic Thoughts and Trigeminal Pain: How Your Mind Influences Nerve Pain

Author: Natalia · Date: 2025-12-02 · 5 min read
Category: Living with Pain

In trigeminal neuralgia and painful trigeminal neuropathy, pain can appear suddenly, intensely, and often without warning. It is only natural that along with the pain come tension, worry, and mistrust of your own body.

In these situations, the thoughts that arise automatically play an enormous role — they usually appear faster than we can even notice them. They can support us... but they can also unnecessarily amplify fear, tension, and pain.

That is why it is so important to understand how emotions form in response to different signals from the body — and how we can influence them.

What are automatic thoughts?

Automatic thoughts are brief, instantaneous mental comments that:

  • Appear in a fraction of a second after a situation occurs
  • Often stem from past difficult experiences with pain
  • Can be highly negative or catastrophic
  • Have an enormous impact on emotional reactions and muscle tension
  • Can increase the sensitivity of the nervous system

Examples:

  • A gentle breeze on the face → "Oh no, this could trigger an attack..."
  • Mild discomfort → "It's starting, it's going to get worse..."
  • Stronger discomfort → "I won't be able to talk, this might provoke the pain."

These thoughts appear automatically — and they are not your fault.

But you can learn to notice them and reframe them, so they don't add suffering on top of the pain.

Thoughts create emotions — and emotions influence pain

Our brain responds not only to what is happening, but primarily to how we interpret it. Especially if in the past the pain was intense, sudden, or hard to control.

Example:

Imagine you're drinking warm tea and feel a slight tingling near your cheek.

If you think: "It's probably nothing, it'll pass in a moment" — you are likely to remain emotionally calm.

If you think: "This is definitely the start of an attack, I'm in for a nightmare" — fear appears, tension builds, you start scanning your face, and stress takes over.

This matters enormously, because emotions and muscle tension intensify neuropathic pain.

A stressed nervous system = a more sensitive nervous system.

3 steps to understanding your reaction

This is a simple method that helps you notice what exactly happens between a stimulus and your experience of pain and emotion.

Step 1: What happened?

A stimulus, a situation, a signal from the body — large or small.

Step 2: What went through my mind?

The automatic interpretation, usually unconscious. Often it is a fear of recurrence, an expectation of the worst, or an assessment of the situation through the lens of previous pain episodes.

Step 3: How did it affect me?

Emotions, physical reactions, muscle tension, behavior.

Example:

1. What happened?

During a conversation, you felt a brief "jolt" in the jaw area.

2. What went through your mind?

"I need to stop talking, because it's probably going to get worse. This could be the start of an attack."

3. How did it affect you?

Emotions: fear, tension, a sense of threat.

Body: facial and neck muscles tighten automatically, breathing speeds up, you become hypervigilant to every signal from your face.

Behavior: you stop the conversation, avoid speaking further, monitor the pain.

These reactions are completely natural — but they can unnecessarily amplify nerve hypersensitivity.

Why does working with thoughts help specifically in neuralgia and neuropathy?

Because with this type of pain:

  • The nervous system becomes more vigilant and reactive
  • The brain is quicker to search for potential "threats"
  • Negative thoughts can intensify reflexive tension in the muscles of the face and neck
  • And this in turn can provoke or increase pain

Noticing and softening automatic thoughts:

  • Reduces muscle tension
  • Decreases vigilance and face-monitoring
  • Lowers nervous system arousal
  • Helps restore a sense of control
  • Can reduce the intensity of flare-ups

This is not a method for instantly eliminating pain, but it is a method for limiting what pain tends to "build around itself" — namely fear, tension, and stress.

What does neurobiology say about this?

Sometimes the emotion appears first (for example, fear), before you even have time to think.

But it is your thoughts that determine whether that emotion calms down or spirals.

And a spiraling emotion = greater tension = greater nervous system sensitivity = greater pain.

That is why:

  • Calming the body
  • Changing automatic thoughts
  • Reducing catastrophizing

...has a real impact on how strongly you experience trigeminal neuralgia or painful trigeminal neuropathy.

Summary

  • Automatic thoughts are natural — but sometimes they are extremely negative.
  • They can amplify tension and increase the sensitivity of the trigeminal nerve.
  • The "3 steps to understanding your reaction" model helps you recognize your own patterns.
  • Changing your interpretation affects emotions, the body, and the perception of pain.
  • Neurobiology confirms: calmer thoughts → a calmer nervous system → less intense pain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

What is the difference between neuralgia and neuropathy?

In simple terms: neuralgia primarily involves paroxysmal pain along a nerve, usually without loss of sensation. Neuropathy involves nerve damage that more often causes constant pain along with numbness and sensory loss.

Do regular painkillers help with neuropathic pain?

Usually not. Neuropathic pain responds poorly to paracetamol or anti-inflammatory drugs. Instead, medications that act on nerves are used, such as anticonvulsants like carbamazepine.
