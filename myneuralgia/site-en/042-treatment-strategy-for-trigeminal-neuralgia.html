Treatment Strategy for Trigeminal Neuralgia — From Medications to Surgery

Author: Natalia · Date: 2025-10-15 · 3 min read
Category: Medication & Supplements

This article is based on information from the book "Neuralgie twarzowe" (Facial Neuralgias) by I. Domitrz, W. Kozubski, J. Kochanowski, A. Stępień.

Treating trigeminal neuralgia is challenging, and in many cases may be only partially effective. Treatment success depends on many factors: misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatment, doses that are too low, and delays in surgical intervention.

Treatment Strategy

Isolated pain attacks do not always require pharmacological treatment.

Exacerbations require a multidirectional approach: pharmacotherapy, psychological support, and adequate hydration.

In cases of severe attacks, hospitalization may be necessary for medication adjustment and dose optimization.

1. Long-Term Pharmacotherapy — First-Line Treatment

Carbamazepine

The drug of choice in classical trigeminal neuralgia.

  • Dose: 200–1200 mg/day, divided, gradually increased.
  • Mechanism: sodium channel blockade, activation of GABA receptors.
  • Efficacy: 70–75% of patients.
  • Possible side effects: drowsiness, dizziness, nausea, hyponatremia, allergic reactions (Stevens-Johnson syndrome).
  • Monitoring of blood count and sodium levels is required.

Oxcarbazepine

  • Similar efficacy to carbamazepine with a better tolerability profile.
  • Dose: 600–1800 mg/day (exceptionally up to 2700 mg).
  • Side effects: rash, hyponatremia, bone demineralization.

2. Second-Line Medications

  • Lamotrigine — used in refractory cases, dose: 25–400 mg/day, gradually increased.
  • Baclofen — muscle relaxant that also relieves pain, dose: 60–80 mg/day, 3–4 times daily.
  • Pimozide — an antipsychotic, rarely used due to side effects, dose: 2–12 mg/day.

3. Third-Line and Adjunctive Medications

  • Gabapentin — inhibits excitatory neurotransmitter release, dose: 1800–3600 mg/day.
  • Pregabalin — similar to gabapentin, dose: 150–600 mg/day, well tolerated.
  • Topiramate — blocks sodium channels, dose: 100–500 mg/day.

Additionally, topical treatments are used: lidocaine patches, capsaicin cream, intranasal medications (sumatriptan, tizanidine), and novel substances under investigation, such as vixotrigine.

Important note regarding contraception: Some anticonvulsants (e.g., carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin) may reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraception. If taking these medications, discuss additional or alternative contraceptive methods with your doctor.

4. Surgical Treatment

Indicated for patients refractory to pharmacotherapy — after trialing at least 3 medications.

Surgical methods:

  1. Trigeminal nerve lesioning (transection, alcohol, radiofrequency, cryotherapy).
  2. Gasserian ganglion lesioning.
  3. Trigeminal nerve root lesioning.
  4. Microvascular decompression (MVD) — the most commonly performed procedure, effective in 80–90% of patients, removing vascular compression from the nerve.
    • Advantages: improved quality of life, relatively low invasiveness.
    • Disadvantages: cost, possible transient sensory loss or paresthesias.

5. Multimodal Approach

Treatment combines pharmacology, psychological therapy, rehabilitation, nerve blocks, cryotherapy, acupuncture, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.

In postherpetic neuralgia, early use of antiviral medications and effective pain control are important to reduce the risk of chronic pain.

Summary

Trigeminal neuralgia requires an individualized, stepwise approach:

  1. Diagnosis — pain assessment, MRI, exclusion of dental causes.
  2. Pharmacological treatment — medication adjustment, monitoring of effects and side effects.
  3. Surgical treatment — when pharmacology does not bring results.
  4. Psychological and multimodal support — improving quality of life and treatment effectiveness.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

How does carbamazepine work for trigeminal neuralgia?

Carbamazepine is the first-line medication for trigeminal neuralgia. In a large proportion of patients, it provides effective pain control. It requires gradual dose adjustment and medical supervision.

What is the difference between gabapentin and pregabalin?

Both are anticonvulsant medications used for neuropathic pain. They differ in absorption and dosing characteristics. The choice and dosage should be determined by a physician.

What is neurovascular conflict (vascular compression)?

This is the most common cause of classical trigeminal neuralgia — a blood vessel compresses the nerve root at the base of the brain, damaging its myelin sheath and triggering pain.

What is microvascular decompression (MVD)?

MVD is a neurosurgical procedure in which the vessel compressing the trigeminal nerve is moved away. In eligible patients, it provides the most durable pain relief. The decision is made by the treating team.
