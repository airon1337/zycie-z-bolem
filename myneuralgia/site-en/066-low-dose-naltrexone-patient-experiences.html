Low Dose Naltrexone (LDN) — Patient Experiences and What the Research Shows

Author: Natalia · Date: 2025-11-24 · 4 min read
Category: Medication & Supplements

Low Dose Naltrexone (LDN) is attracting growing interest among patients with various conditions, including neuropathic pain. Although it is still used "off-label," many patients describe it as a therapy that can significantly improve quality of life.

Among patients, reports emerge of reduced pain, decreased inflammation, improved immune function, but equally often they highlight difficulties with tolerance, the need to adjust dosing, and the highly individual nature of responses.

This article is based exclusively on real accounts from people taking LDN (from internet forums), their observations, challenges, hopes, and side effects, alongside current medical knowledge.

This is not a medical text or treatment guide, but a collection of patient experiences that may help others understand what to expect during LDN therapy and how varied individual responses can be.

Patient Experiences — Observed Benefits

Reduced Neuropathic and Chronic Pain

Many patients describe significant improvement:

"LDN is the only medication that lets me function. Without it, the pain would have broken me."

These observations are consistent with pilot study results in fibromyalgia, where LDN reduced pain perception by 30–40% compared to placebo.

Improved Immunity and Immune System Stabilization

Patients report getting sick less often after several months of therapy:

"Before LDN I constantly had sinus infections. Since 2012, not once."

Research suggests LDN may modulate microglial activity and cytokines (TNF-α, TGF-β), reducing inflammation.

Better Tolerance of Facial / Nerve Pain

Individual stories from patients with painful trigeminal neuropathy who experienced pain reduction after many months have appeared:

"At first it was hard, but after 5 months I noticed attacks were shorter and weaker."

Although large randomized controlled trials for TN do not exist, LDN's mechanism of action (glial cells, inflammation, cytokines) suggests therapeutic potential.

Most Common Side Effects

Sleep Disturbances

Insomnia, waking after 3–5 hours, vivid realistic dreams.

"I was taking 1.5 mg in the evening. I would wake up every 7 hours, like clockwork."

This is consistent with the literature: naltrexone affects endorphins, which can alter sleep patterns in the first weeks.

Gastrointestinal Disturbances

Nausea, cramps, bloating.

"After increasing to 3 mg, cramps and gas started."

Heart Palpitations

Rare, but reported by some patients:

"I felt like I'd had several strong espressos."

According to the LDN Research Trust, these are transient symptoms usually related to too-rapid dose adjustments.

Bladder Irritation

Patients with a sensitive bladder sometimes react even to small doses:

"Above 1 mg my bladder starts hurting. At 1 mg everything returns to normal."

What the Research Shows

The best data come from fibromyalgia: in two randomized trials, pain reduction of 30–40% was observed, with improved mood and quality of life and good tolerability.

Mechanism of action:

LDN does not work like a classical opioid. Instead it works through:

  • brief opioid receptor blockade (endorphin rebound effect),
  • modulation of microglia (TLR-4),
  • reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines,
  • neuroprotective effects.

These mechanisms are relevant in neuralgia and neuropathies.

Safety:

LDN is considered:

  • well tolerated,
  • low risk profile,
  • non-addictive,
  • without withdrawal effects.

Problems more often arise from inappropriate dosing.

Relevance for Trigeminal Neuralgia / Neuropathy

LDN may be a valuable option for people with trigeminal neuralgia or painful trigeminal neuropathy when standard medications (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, gabapentin) are ineffective or poorly tolerated.

LDN's mechanism (reduced microglial activity and inflammatory cytokines) is linked to the pathophysiology of painful trigeminal neuropathy.

Key principles for trigeminal pain:

  • Start with very low doses (0.25–0.5 mg).
  • Increase slowly, even every 2–3 weeks.
  • Be patient: effects may take several months to appear.
  • If pain worsens after a dose increase, step back rather than increase further.
  • Patients taking opioids must consult their doctor about LDN (interaction risk).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is trigeminal neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic facial pain condition. It manifests as sudden, severe, usually one-sided pain attacks — often described as an electric shock. A single attack typically lasts from a few seconds to about two minutes.

What is the difference between neuralgia and neuropathy?

In simple terms: neuralgia primarily involves paroxysmal pain along a nerve, usually without loss of sensation. Neuropathy involves nerve damage that more often causes constant pain along with numbness and sensory loss.

Do regular painkillers help with neuropathic pain?

Usually not. Neuropathic pain responds poorly to paracetamol or anti-inflammatory drugs. Instead, medications that act on nerves are used, such as anticonvulsants like carbamazepine.

How does carbamazepine work for trigeminal neuralgia?

Carbamazepine is the first-line medication for trigeminal neuralgia. In a large proportion of patients, it provides effective pain control. It requires gradual dose adjustment and medical supervision.

What is the difference between gabapentin and pregabalin?

Both are anticonvulsant medications used for neuropathic pain. They differ in absorption and dosing characteristics. The choice and dosage should be determined by a physician.
