Trigeminal nerve pain is one of the most distressing and difficult-to-bear types of pain. It can take different forms:

- **Neuralgia** — sudden, piercing, "electric shock-like" pain lasting seconds to minutes, often triggered by speaking, chewing, touching the face, or brushing teeth.
- **Neuropathy** — continuous, burning, stinging pain, frequently accompanied by numbness, hypersensitivity, or altered sensation.

These two presentations require similar specialists, but not always the same treatment. Here is a step-by-step guide.

## First Steps — Where to Seek Help

**1. Neurologist** — the primary specialist.

- Will conduct a history and neurological examination.
- May order an **MRI** of the brain with an appropriate protocol to rule out vascular compression of the nerve, tumors, or other lesions.
- Will propose pharmacological treatment (e.g., **carbamazepine**, **oxcarbazepine** — the classic first-line medications).

**2. Dentist / oral and maxillofacial surgeon** — if the pain seems "dental."

- Important, because neuralgia is often confused with toothache, and many patients see a dentist first.
- Allows dental causes to be ruled out.

**3. Anesthesiologist / pain medicine specialist (pain clinic)**

- Responsible for advanced pain management: nerve blocks, injections, radiofrequency procedures.
- Pain clinics offer interdisciplinary care, particularly when pain is refractory to medications.

**4. Neurosurgeon / stereotactic neurosurgery centers** — if conservative treatment fails.

- Possible procedures: microvascular decompression (MVD), percutaneous procedures (radiofrequency, balloon compression, glycerol injections), and stereotactic radiosurgery (e.g., Gamma Knife).
- In analyses, MVD provides the best long-term outcomes, but the choice of method depends on patient age, cause of pain, and overall health.

## Important Distinction: Neuralgia vs. Neuropathy Treatment

The above procedures are typically used in trigeminal neuralgia — where we have paroxysmal, piercing, brief pains usually related to vascular compression of the nerve root.

Why?

- **MVD** works when the cause of pain is a vessel compressing the nerve. In neuropathy (e.g., post-traumatic, post-dental surgery, post-herpetic), this mechanism is absent, so the effect would be poor.
- **Percutaneous procedures and Gamma Knife** also target interruption of pain conduction in classical neuralgia. In neuropathy, where pain is chronic, burning, and related to nerve damage itself, effectiveness is significantly lower.

**In neuropathy, the main treatment approaches are:**

- pharmacotherapy (**gabapentin**, **pregabalin**, **baclofen**),
- nerve blocks, local injections, sometimes botulinum toxin,
- supportive therapies (chronic pain management, psychological support, rehabilitation),
- in exceptional, very difficult cases — neuromodulation (e.g., stimulation of the nerve or pain centers).

## Pharmacological Treatment Summary

- **First-line:** **carbamazepine**, **oxcarbazepine**.
- **Other options:** **gabapentin**, **pregabalin**, **baclofen** — selection depends on tolerance and clinical presentation.
- **Botulinum toxin (Botox)** — growing body of evidence for effectiveness in some patients, particularly refractory cases; however, still considered an additional or experimental option in some centers.

## Invasive and Supportive Procedures

- Nerve blocks, local injections, radiofrequency
- Microvascular decompression (MVD)
- Gamma Knife and other radiosurgical techniques
- Neuromodulation (stimulators), cryoablation

## Additional Support

**Psychologist / psychiatrist** — chronic pain frequently leads to anxiety and depression; psychological support is part of therapy. It is crucial that a patient never doubts the reality of their pain — it is not "made up" or "in your head."

Pain psychology demonstrates that the brain actively responds to pain: it processes nerve signals, and additionally, emotions, stress, and tension can amplify pain perception. The greater the psychological burden, the stronger the pain experience may become.

**Physiotherapy, relaxation techniques, yoga, mindfulness** — they do not eliminate the cause but help reduce tension and cope with chronic pain.

**Support groups / patient organizations** — sharing experiences and practical advice.

## Practical Tips

- Keep a pain diary — record the timing of attacks, their character, triggering factors, and medication effectiveness.
- Seek multidisciplinary centers — collaboration between a neurologist, pain specialist, neurosurgeon, and dentist yields the best results.
