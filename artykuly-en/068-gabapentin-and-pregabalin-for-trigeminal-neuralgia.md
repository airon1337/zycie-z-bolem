**Gabapentin** and **pregabalin** are antiepileptic drugs commonly used to treat trigeminal neuralgia and other types of neuropathic pain. They work by calming overexcited neurons, which helps reduce pain signaling.

In practice, many patients notice significant improvement after starting treatment, especially during the first few weeks when symptoms can clearly subside. At the same time, for some individuals these medications do not provide the expected relief from the start. This usually results from individual differences in nervous system function, the degree of nerve damage, coexisting muscle tension, or genetic sensitivity to gabapentinoids.

Over time, however, a phenomenon of diminished effectiveness may occur. The body gradually becomes accustomed to the presence of the medication, and the nervous system adapts, weakening its effect. In some patients the drug begins to work only temporarily, and in others, though less commonly, it stops working altogether. This is known as developing tolerance. In certain situations, we even refer to tachyphylaxis, a rapid loss of drug effect where the medication works well initially but then becomes increasingly less noticeable.

**Gabapentin** works by reducing excessive nerve discharges in the central nervous system. However, research shows that its effectiveness varies considerably among patients. Approximately 30–40% of individuals experience significant relief, but not everyone responds to the same degree. Many factors influence how strongly and how long the medication works, including the condition of the nerve, disease duration, individual sensitivity, stress levels, sleep quality, and muscle tension around the face and neck.

It can happen that the drug stops being effective at the same dose, requiring an increase. Sometimes dose escalation does not yield the expected improvement or causes drowsiness, dizziness, or balance problems. In these situations, physicians often decide to switch medications — for example, to pregabalin — or introduce combination therapy.

In some patients, **gabapentin** will not work from the beginning. In those cases, a different medication or complementary methods must be tried. Alternatives may include **pregabalin**, **carbamazepine**, **oxcarbazepine**, **lamotrigine**, nerve blocks, neuromodulation, or even surgical procedures in the most severe cases of trigeminal neuralgia.

**Pregabalin**, although its mechanism of action is similar, often proves more effective in patients who did not respond to gabapentin or in whom its effect diminished over time. It is absorbed more consistently, provides relief more quickly, and is often better tolerated. Many patients describe its effect as more "even" throughout the day, which can be crucial for sudden, lancinating neuralgic pain.

In the practical management of trigeminal neuralgia, **gabapentin** remains one of the most commonly used medications. However, the reality is that many patients experience a return of symptoms after an initial period of improvement.

At that point, a physician may adjust the dose, switch to pregabalin, or add complementary treatment.

Supportive therapies are playing an increasingly important role, including craniosacral therapy, neural mobilization, work with facial and neck muscle tension, relaxation techniques, and autonomic nervous system regulation training. These approaches are particularly valuable because they can reduce tension around the nerve, improve muscle function, and often limit the need for dose increases.

Research findings and clinical reviews emphasize that the response to gabapentin and pregabalin is highly individual, and treating chronic neuropathic pain requires a flexible, patient-centered approach.

Modern treatment should increasingly prioritize personalized therapy — combining pharmacotherapy with physical therapy, lifestyle modifications, and nervous system support — to achieve the most lasting improvement in function and quality of life.
