Treatment of trigeminal neuralgia and trigeminal neuropathy begins with pharmacotherapy. The most important role is played by anticonvulsants, which reduce the hyperexcitability of nerve fibers and interrupt pain attacks.

Antidepressants also serve an important function. They are not prescribed because the pain has a psychological basis, but because of their proven analgesic properties. They modulate conduction within the nervous system and change the way the body perceives pain.

For this reason, they are used in all forms of trigeminal neuralgia — both paroxysmal and continuous. They are frequently combined with anticonvulsants, which strengthens the therapeutic effect while allowing lower doses of each medication.

## Anticonvulsants

They stabilize neuronal membranes and block the abnormal "electrical discharges" that cause sudden pain attacks.

**First-line choices:**

- **Carbamazepine** — the classic, most extensively studied option.
- **Oxcarbazepine** — similar mechanism, often better tolerated.

**Alternatives and second-line options:**

- **Lamotrigine** — particularly useful for continuous pain.
- **Baclofen** — a muscle relaxant that also modulates pain conduction.
- **Gabapentin** and **Pregabalin** — effective for constant neuropathic pain.
- Topiramate — used in more difficult cases.
- Phenytoin — less common today, but still occasionally used in refractory neuralgia.

## Antidepressants

They modify pain signaling in the brain and spinal cord — functioning as a kind of "pain switch."

**Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):**

- Amitriptyline
- Nortriptyline
- Imipramine

**Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs):**

- Duloxetine
- Venlafaxine

## Combination Therapy — Even More Effective

Many patients benefit from combining anticonvulsants with antidepressants.

Why?

- Both drug classes target different pain mechanisms.
- Lower doses of each can be used, reducing the risk of side effects.
- The analgesic effect is often stronger and more sustained than with monotherapy.
